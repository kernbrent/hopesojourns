Senior and executive leadership curriculum

Leadership Transformation

Heart transformation expressed through integrity, compassion, and responsibility in how you lead staff, serve clients, and work with peers.

This curriculum guides leaders before, during, and after a corporate humanitarian journey. Encounters with people, their strengths, and stories of hope or heart transformation can invite leaders to examine their own hearts. Facilitated reflection connects that examination with choices others can experience at work.

Eight 90-minute modules provide 12 hours of facilitated learning, in addition to partner-led service, travel, and individual reflection. The suggested sequence includes two preparation sessions, four sessions during the journey, and two after returning. Adapt timing with the hosts; service priorities and appropriate rest take precedence. Schedule three additional 30-minute accountability check-ins at days 30, 60, and 90.

Before you begin

Facilitator preparation and learning outcomes

Use a facilitator comfortable leading executive discussions about power, feedback, ethics, and personal reflection. Plan groups small enough for each leader to contribute without pressure; split into pairs or groups of three for practice. The local host leads service and community relationships. The facilitator leads learning; the company sponsor supports the agreed workplace follow-through.

By the end of the curriculum leaders will be able to

  • Identify a motive or assumption that affects their leadership and describe its effect on another person.
  • Listen, summarize, and check understanding before offering a solution.
  • Recognize power differences and protect consent in service and storytelling.
  • Combine respectful treatment with clear expectations and accountability.
  • Work through a client or peer conflict using an explicit integrity decision process.
  • Practice and review one observable commitment each for staff, clients, and peers over 90 days.

Prepare the setting

Meet with the company sponsor and local partner before scheduling. Agree on service priorities, accessibility and participation needs, available time, language support, roles, conduct, costs, and a concern-reporting contact. Discuss the Christian foundation openly before participation. Confirm whether optional Scripture reflection fits the group and provide an equally valid quiet-reflection option.

Bring a journal or private notes for each participant, the host-approved service brief, the session questions, and the practice-plan prompts. No participant needs to record names, images, or identifying details of people served. Arrange a private place for discussion away from community activities.

Protect dignity and choice

Invite reflection without requiring personal disclosure. Agree not to repeat others’ stories; explain that confidentiality has limits where safety or organizational reporting obligations apply. A manager should not solicit identifiable direct-report feedback in a group session. Do not use journals or disclosure as performance ratings. Agree beforehand what aggregate program feedback the company sponsor will receive.

Never make a community member’s testimony, photograph, or claimed transformation a learning requirement. Obtain specific, freely given permission for any story use, including its audience and purpose. Ask the host how to receive and address concerns. If an activity causes distress, allow the participant to pause, decline, or speak privately; use established support and emergency procedures where needed. Facilitation remains within leadership learning rather than personal diagnosis or treatment.

Lead a daily reflection

After service, allow a brief pause, invite factual observations, explore assumptions, ask what the encounter reveals about the leader’s own choices, and close with one practice. Ask, “What do we still not know?” before drawing conclusions. Reframe stereotypes and honor each person’s agency. Keep optional faith discussion invitational and focus application on the participant’s own conduct.

Module 1 · 90 minutes

The heart behind your leadership

Preparation · two to four weeks before travel

Leadership objective: Recognize how ambition, fear, compassion, and the need for control influence your use of authority.

Opening question

Recall a recent decision that affected someone with less organizational power. What did you want to accomplish, and what were you trying to protect in yourself?

Facilitator teaching

A leadership title gives you authority, but people experience your leadership through attention, tone, decisions, and follow-through. Heart transformation begins when you examine the motives beneath those behaviors. Compassion does not remove responsibility for performance; it changes how you exercise that responsibility.

Session sequence

  1. 15 minutes · Establish the learning agreement. Invite each leader to name a hope for the journey and a boundary for personal sharing. Agree that participants may pass, keep others’ stories private, and question decisions without attacking people.
  2. 20 minutes · Map a decision. Individually trace a real decision: situation, emotion, assumption, action, and effect on someone else. Separate what you know from what you inferred. Name a value that guided you and one you neglected.
  3. 30 minutes · Seek a different perspective. In pairs, describe the decision without identifying staff or clients. The listener asks what the affected person might have experienced. Trade roles; ask permission before offering advice.
  4. 15 minutes · Set a baseline. Choose one observable habit involving staff, one involving clients, and one involving peers. Rate its consistency from 1 to 5 using a recent example. Before travel, invite voluntary feedback from a trusted colleague; keep their comments private.
  5. 10 minutes · Choose a learning intention. Write: “When I feel __, I tend to __. During this journey I will practice __.” Identify someone who can respectfully challenge you.

Reflection questions

  • Where do I confuse being needed with being helpful?
  • What do people risk when they disagree with me?
  • Which leadership value costs me something to practice?

Takeaway: A private leadership baseline, three relational habits to observe, and one learning intention.

Practice between sessions: Before the next session, hold one conversation in which you ask for feedback, summarize what you heard, and respond without explaining away the concern.

Optional Christian reflection: Psalm 139:23–24; Mark 10:42–45. Invite participants to consider how the passage informs their own leadership; offer quiet reflection as an alternative.

Module 2 · 90 minutes

Serve with integrity and cultural humility

Preparation · one to two weeks before travel

Leadership objective: Enter a community as a guest who respects local authority, consent, boundaries, and the contribution of others.

Opening question

When you enter a setting with money, influence, or professional expertise, what might make it difficult for others to tell you no?

Facilitator teaching

Integrity requires consistency between the purpose of the visit and the choices made on the ground. Local leaders understand needs and capacity that visitors may miss. A person’s hardship does not create permission to collect their story. Service and assistance remain available regardless of someone’s willingness to share, be photographed, or participate in spiritual reflection.

Session sequence

  1. 15 minutes · Hear the partner brief. The facilitator shares the host-approved priorities, boundaries, service roles, and reporting contact. Ask what useful contribution looks like from the partner’s perspective. Record unanswered questions for the host; do not assume permission.
  2. 20 minutes · Examine power. List what your team brings and what local people bring. Identify how status, language, resources, and time pressures could silence a concern. Decide how a host can pause or redirect the team.
  3. 30 minutes · Rehearse an integrity decision. Scenario: an executive wants a photograph and an emotional testimony for a company presentation; a host appears hesitant. In groups, practice stopping the request, asking privately about consent, and choosing an alternative that does not pressure anyone.
  4. 15 minutes · Agree on conduct. Write a team agreement covering photographs, stories, gifts, promises, confidentiality, appropriate tasks, and respect for a refusal. Name who receives concerns and how the team will follow host procedures.
  5. 10 minutes · Commit to a boundary. Each leader names one boundary they will uphold even if it disappoints a colleague or reduces publicity.

Reflection questions

  • What expertise do I need to receive before offering my own?
  • How will I make refusal safe and respectful?
  • Would I describe a person this way if they were in the room?

Takeaway: A shared service and storytelling agreement, a list of host questions, and a clear route for reporting concerns.

Practice between sessions: Ask the local partner what visitors often misunderstand and what respectful support would look like. Receive the answer without defending previous practices.

Optional Christian reflection: Micah 6:8; Philippians 2:3–4. Invite participants to consider how the passage informs their own leadership; offer quiet reflection as an alternative.

Module 3 · 90 minutes

See the person and listen with care

During the journey · after the first service experience

Leadership objective: Practice attentive listening while recognizing the dignity, agency, and strengths of each person.

Opening question

Recall a moment when someone gave you their full attention. What did they do that helped you feel seen?

Facilitator teaching

Listening begins with presence and permission. A service encounter is a relationship between people who each have something to contribute. Questions should make room for what a person wishes to share. They do not need to disclose hardship, explain their past, or reassure visitors. Leaders can learn from someone’s insight and strengths without knowing their most painful experiences.

Session sequence

  1. 15 minutes · Notice before interpreting. Write three observations from service without names or identifying details. Underline interpretation words such as “unmotivated” or “grateful” and replace them with what you actually saw or heard.
  2. 20 minutes · Practice listening. In pairs, discuss an ordinary leadership challenge. Listen for five minutes without interruption. Ask an open question, summarize, and check whether you understood. Reverse roles; do not use a community member’s private story.
  3. 30 minutes · Review an encounter. Using an encounter that can be discussed without exposing anyone, ask: What did the person choose to share? What strengths did I notice? What did I assume? What remains unknown? The facilitator interrupts labels or speculation and invites a more accurate description.
  4. 15 minutes · Transfer to staff and clients. Scenario: a staff member’s missed deadline or a client complaint triggers a quick judgment. Rehearse an opening question, an accurate summary, and a clear next step that preserves accountability.
  5. 10 minutes · Name one change. Choose a meeting or conversation back at work where you will practice listening before proposing a solution.

Reflection questions

  • Did I listen to understand, to fix, or to demonstrate competence?
  • Whose strengths have I overlooked because I focused on their needs?
  • What would I ask if I were willing to be surprised?

Takeaway: A listening practice and a specific workplace conversation in which to use it.

Practice between sessions: With the host’s guidance, contribute to the next service activity without directing it. Notice when you want to interrupt, solve, or take over.

Optional Christian reflection: James 1:19; Luke 10:25–37. Invite participants to consider how the passage informs their own leadership; offer quiet reflection as an alternative.

Module 4 · 90 minutes

Let encounters examine your heart

During the journey · after further service and relationship building

Leadership objective: Connect encounters with hope, resilience, and freely shared stories of change to honest examination of your own motives and behavior.

Opening question

What encounter has stayed with you? Describe what you experienced without naming someone or revealing a story you do not have permission to repeat.

Facilitator teaching

Hearing how someone’s heart or life is changing can invite a leader to reconsider their own priorities. Sometimes the invitation comes through courage, forgiveness, generosity, or perseverance rather than a dramatic testimony. The community does not owe visitors a transformation story. A leader’s emotional response is a beginning for reflection; lasting change requires a different choice practiced over time.

Session sequence

  1. 15 minutes · Make space for reflection. Allow quiet writing, a walk where appropriate, or optional prayer. Ask participants to note what moved them, unsettled them, or exposed a familiar defensive response.
  2. 20 minutes · Separate encounter from projection. Use five prompts: What happened? What meaning did I attach to it? What value did it touch? What in me resisted? What might a more faithful response require? Distinguish facts from conclusions about another person’s inner life.
  3. 30 minutes · Share with permission. In groups of three, share only what you choose about your own learning. Listeners ask clarifying questions without diagnosing, correcting beliefs, or demanding disclosure. No one repeats another participant’s reflection outside the group.
  4. 15 minutes · Translate emotion into a choice. Scenario: you return moved by another person’s forgiveness, yet routinely humiliate colleagues who make mistakes. Identify a concrete change that combines responsibility, repair, and respectful treatment.
  5. 10 minutes · Write a heart-to-practice commitment. Complete: “I have been protecting __. I want to become more __. The next time __ happens, I will __.” Choose language that can lead to action.

Reflection questions

  • What did I receive from someone I expected only to help?
  • Where do my stated values and habitual reactions diverge?
  • What would change if compassion shaped my next difficult decision?

Takeaway: A private heart-to-practice commitment with one trigger and one different response.

Practice between sessions: Try the new response during the journey. Record what you actually did and what you still need to practice; do not rate someone else’s transformation.

Optional Christian reflection: Ezekiel 36:26; Romans 12:2. Invite participants to consider how the passage informs their own leadership; offer quiet reflection as an alternative.

Module 5 · 90 minutes

Use power to build trust and responsibility

During the journey · following a shared service task

Leadership objective: Recognize the effect of positional power and practice accountability that protects dignity and invites honest input.

Opening question

Think of a time you were corrected by someone with authority. What helped you accept responsibility, and what made it harder?

Facilitator teaching

Leaders affect whether people can raise a concern, admit uncertainty, or acknowledge a mistake. Trust grows when a leader’s response is proportionate, clear, and consistent. Compassion and accountability belong together: a person can be treated with respect while expectations, consequences, and follow-through remain explicit. Local service offers an opportunity to receive direction and accept correction.

Session sequence

  1. 15 minutes · Review receiving direction. Describe a moment when a host or teammate redirected your work. Notice whether you asked a question, defended yourself, complied outwardly, or changed course.
  2. 20 minutes · Map whose voice counts. Draw a real workplace decision and list who gives input, who decides, who bears the consequences, and who can appeal. Identify one person or group affected but unheard.
  3. 30 minutes · Practice a difficult conversation. Scenario: a capable manager publicly dismisses a junior colleague’s concern, then asks you to protect their reputation. Role-play addressing the behavior, asking about its effect, restating the standard, and agreeing on a repair. Repeat with clearer language and less defensiveness.
  4. 15 minutes · Design a safer response. Choose one way to invite concerns without retaliation. Explain what can remain confidential and what must be escalated under existing workplace procedures. Agree to report back on what you heard and what action you took.
  5. 10 minutes · Commit to follow-through. Set a date to revisit a concern or decision with the people affected. Name what you will do if you discover you were wrong.

Reflection questions

  • When does my urgency silence useful disagreement?
  • Do I offer the same respect to a junior employee as to a board member?
  • What will people see me do when my own behavior falls short?

Takeaway: A revised decision or feedback practice with an owner, a response standard, and a review date.

Practice between sessions: Invite a teammate to identify one way your influence has helped and one way it may have constrained the group. Thank them and choose a response.

Optional Christian reflection: Mark 10:42–45; Proverbs 15:1. Invite participants to consider how the passage informs their own leadership; offer quiet reflection as an alternative.

Module 6 · 90 minutes

Lead clients and peers with integrity

During the journey · before departure

Leadership objective: Apply compassion and ethical judgment when business pressure creates competing obligations.

Opening question

Where are you most tempted to soften the truth: to protect revenue, preserve status, avoid conflict, or keep a promise you cannot fulfill?

Facilitator teaching

Integrity becomes visible when leaders name limits, disclose uncertainty, share credit, and take responsibility for harm. Service invites leaders to see people beyond their usefulness to an outcome. At work, that means considering the client who depends on your promise, the peer who absorbs a rushed decision, and the employee asked to deliver it. Honest relationships still require clear decisions and difficult conversations.

Session sequence

  1. 15 minutes · Notice a shared dependency. Recall a service task that depended on another person’s knowledge or contribution. What changed when you recognized that dependence?
  2. 20 minutes · Apply a decision lens. Ask: Who is affected? What do we know and not know? Who has not been heard? What promise or value is at stake? What options are available? What can I explain honestly to everyone affected?
  3. 30 minutes · Work two executive cases. Case A: a major client wants a delivery date your team believes is unrealistic. Rehearse a truthful conversation with options and a firm follow-up. Case B: a peer takes credit for cross-team work and seeks your silence. Practice a direct, respectful conversation that names contributions and a repair. Discuss who bears the cost of each option.
  4. 15 minutes · Test a real decision. Privately apply the lens to a current decision. Use an anonymized version with a peer. Ask for challenge rather than agreement; follow existing company escalation processes where needed.
  5. 10 minutes · Close with gratitude and responsibility. Name what local partners contributed to your learning, what you can responsibly share, and what remains your own work to do after returning.

Reflection questions

  • Which commitment needs to be clarified before it becomes a broken promise?
  • What do I ask others to absorb so I can appear successful?
  • How can I challenge a peer while preserving their dignity?

Takeaway: A decision note describing affected people, tradeoffs, next action, and a review date.

Practice between sessions: Before departure, seek host feedback through the agreed channel. Ask what was useful and what should change; do not ask hosts to validate your personal transformation.

Optional Christian reflection: Proverbs 11:3; Matthew 5:37. Invite participants to consider how the passage informs their own leadership; offer quiet reflection as an alternative.

Module 7 · 90 minutes

Bring transformation into everyday leadership

Return to work · within the first week

Leadership objective: Translate journey learning into specific, proportionate commitments involving staff, clients, and peers.

Opening question

Which behavior from before the journey is most likely to return under pressure? What will help you notice it sooner?

Facilitator teaching

Insight becomes credible through repeated behavior and repair. Returning leaders need space to integrate learning without making immediate sweeping changes or asking employees to share their experience. Explain your own commitment in plain language. Seek input on the effect of your leadership, then make a manageable change with a clear review point.

Session sequence

  1. 15 minutes · Revisit the baseline. Read your original intentions and observations. Identify what changed in your understanding and what remains uncertain. Describe a behavior you practiced; avoid declaring yourself transformed.
  2. 20 minutes · Choose three relational commitments. Select one commitment for staff, one for clients, and one for peers. Each needs a trigger, an action, an owner, a date, and evidence others can observe.
  3. 30 minutes · Rehearse the return conversation. Practice saying: “I noticed __ about my leadership. I am working on __. Here is what you can expect. What effect would that have on your work?” The listener names what sounds useful, vague, or burdensome. Revise without asking them to endorse your journey.
  4. 15 minutes · Plan repair and support. Identify a relationship where you owe an acknowledgment or apology. Prepare a specific statement of responsibility and an invitation to discuss repair. Choose an accountability partner and schedule a check-in.
  5. 10 minutes · Protect the first step. Place the first practice on your calendar. Identify a workload or process adjustment that makes it realistic.

Reflection questions

  • What will someone who never traveled with us notice?
  • Which promise is small enough to keep and meaningful enough to matter?
  • Where does an apology need to precede a new initiative?

Takeaway: A 90-day practice plan with one commitment each for staff, clients, and peers, plus an accountability partner.

Practice between sessions: Carry out the first commitment within seven days. Ask one affected person whether the action was useful, making feedback voluntary and safe.

Optional Christian reflection: James 1:22; Colossians 3:12–14. Invite participants to consider how the passage informs their own leadership; offer quiet reflection as an alternative.

Module 8 · 90 minutes

Sustain the change and share responsibility

Return to work · within the first month

Leadership objective: Build a review rhythm that tests leadership change through behavior, feedback, and the effect on others.

Opening question

What evidence would convince a skeptical colleague that your leadership is changing? What evidence would challenge your own assessment?

Facilitator teaching

Transformation is an ongoing practice. A moving story or a high self-rating does not establish sustained change. Use a few relevant observations, invite honest feedback, and notice unintended effects. Employees and partners are not responsible for proving a leader’s growth. Share only the learning and commitments you have permission to discuss.

Session sequence

  1. 15 minutes · Review early practice. Compare your commitments with what you actually did. Note one success, one lapse, and one constraint. Describe behavior before interpreting impact.
  2. 20 minutes · Choose useful evidence. For each commitment, identify one record of practice and one voluntary feedback question. For example: Did I ask for dissent before deciding? Did I explain the client limitation clearly? Did I credit the peer team by name?
  3. 30 minutes · Hold an accountability conversation. In pairs, review a lapse without defensiveness. The partner asks what happened, who was affected, what repair is needed, and what will change. Agree on a next check-in rather than a character judgment.
  4. 15 minutes · Establish the review rhythm. Confirm 30-, 60-, and 90-day check-ins using the practice plan below. Decide what stays private, what can be shared with the company sponsor, and how feedback will be gathered without pressure.
  5. 10 minutes · Commit to continued service. Choose a realistic way to continue supporting people or partners. Confirm any partner commitment before promising money, future visits, or company support.

Reflection questions

  • Where am I changing my behavior, and where am I only changing my language?
  • What feedback have I dismissed because it is uncomfortable?
  • What structure will help me continue when the emotional intensity fades?

Takeaway: A completed review schedule, a small set of evidence, and a revised practice plan with consent-based reporting.

Practice between sessions: At day 90, decide with your accountability partner what to continue, revise, or stop. Recognize unresolved issues and choose the next period of practice.

Optional Christian reflection: Galatians 6:9; Philippians 1:6. Invite participants to consider how the passage informs their own leadership; offer quiet reflection as an alternative.

From insight to practice

Your 90-day leadership practice plan

Make one commitment for each relationship group. Use these examples as a starting point, then choose actions suited to your responsibilities. Keep names and feedback in your private working copy.

Examples of observable leadership commitments
RelationshipPracticeEvidence and feedback
StaffIn the next four team decisions, ask for concerns before stating your preference. Summarize the concern and explain the final decision.Keep a count of the practice. Ask voluntarily: “Was there room to disagree, and did you understand what happened next?”
ClientsFor a difficult commitment, clarify what can be delivered, what is uncertain, and when you will update the client.Review the written commitment and whether the update occurred. Ask whether expectations were clear.
PeersAddress one unresolved cross-team concern directly and acknowledge the other team’s contribution in the next shared review.Record the agreed next step. Ask the peer whether the conversation was fair and useful.

Complete these prompts for each commitment

  1. My intention: What motive or habit am I working to change?
  2. The situation: When and with whom does this behavior arise? Avoid recording unnecessary personal details.
  3. My action: What will I do differently, and how often?
  4. My first date: When will I begin, and what time or support must I arrange?
  5. My evidence: What behavior can I record without monitoring someone else’s private experience?
  6. My feedback: Who may choose to comment, how can they decline safely, and how will I protect their words?
  7. My accountability: Who will review this with me at days 30, 60, and 90?
  8. My repair: If I lapse or cause harm, how will I acknowledge it, address the effect, and adjust?

A useful commitment is specific enough to observe and modest enough to keep. Avoid goals such as “make everyone trust me” or targets that require staff to disclose feelings or praise a leader. Track your conduct and receive feedback as information, not proof of your worth.

Follow-through

Review and accountability

At each additional 30-minute check-in, spend 10 minutes reviewing actual behavior, 10 minutes considering voluntary feedback and effects on others, and 10 minutes deciding on repair or adjustment. Bring your baseline and commitments, with identifying details removed where necessary.

  • Day 30: Did the first practices happen? What made them easier or harder? Simplify an unrealistic commitment and make any needed repair.
  • Day 60: Are practices continuing under pressure? What do staff, clients, or peers say voluntarily? Examine a real decision that tested integrity.
  • Day 90: Compare current habits with the original baseline using examples, not a satisfaction score alone. Decide what to continue and what remains unfinished. Plan the next review.

Review the journey with the local partner

Use the agreed contact and timing to ask whether the service was useful, whether the visit created additional burdens, whether boundaries were respected, and what the partner would change. Make it possible to raise concerns privately. Agree on any next step together. Positive executive feedback does not substitute for the host’s assessment.

Report with integrity

Agree with participants what may be shared with the sponsor. A concise report can describe sessions completed, practices chosen, follow-up participation, and anonymized themes where consent and group size permit. Avoid identifiable staff feedback, private journals, unsupported impact claims, or claims about a community member’s heart. Recognize mixed results and the limits of self-reporting.

Close by asking: “How are people experiencing my leadership differently, what evidence do I have, and what responsibility remains mine?”
